Trade chokepoints have become targets of armed disruption, yet existing work treats such episodes as exogenous hazards. This paper models chokepoint disruption as a strategic choice. In a multi-country model, a disruptor country may pay to raise trade costs between other countries, diverting demand toward itself. Disruption therefore improves the disruptor's terms of trade even absent any geopolitical motive, and the gain grows with the corridor's importance to world trade. I take the model to the 2023–24 Red Sea crisis using a new, network-based measure of logistical distance that captures each country pair's exposure to disruption of the Bab el-Mandeb Strait. Trade fell on exposed pairs, most sharply among Western-aligned pairs, and welfare losses rise with exposure while Iran's own gain is small. Treating the observed campaign as Iran's optimal choice reveals extraordinary leverage: intensifying the campaign slightly would gain Iran $48 to $78 million per year while costing the average Western economy $1.6 to $8.1 billion. Pure self-interest can rationalize the campaign only if it cost Iran under $78 million per year, below even the lowest estimate of Iran's annual support to the Houthis. Rationalizing it therefore requires a strictly positive weight on harming adversaries: in the benchmark calibration, Iran is willing to give up one dollar of its own welfare to destroy roughly $12 of Western welfare.
