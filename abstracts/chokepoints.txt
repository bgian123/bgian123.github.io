Trade chokepoints have become targets of armed disruption, yet existing work treats such episodes as exogenous hazards. This paper models chokepoint disruption as a choice. In an (N+1)-country Armington economy, a disruptor pays a private cost to raise trade costs between other countries, diverting demand toward itself. Disruption improves the disruptor's terms of trade even absent any geopolitical motive, and the gain scales quadratically in the size of the disrupted corridor. I take the model to the 2023–24 Red Sea crisis using a new measure of logistical distance, computed over a global road and maritime network, that yields a continuous, geography-determined measure of each country pair's exposure to closure of the Bab el-Mandeb Strait. Trade fell on exposed dyads, with the response concentrated among Western-aligned pairs. In general equilibrium, welfare losses increase with exposure while Iran's own gains are small. Inverting the disruptor's first-order condition shows why: at the margin, each dollar Iran gains costs the average Western economy between roughly $34 and $103, and pure self-interest can rationalize the campaign only if it cost Iran under $78 million per year, below even the lowest independent estimate of Iran's annual support to the Houthis. A strictly positive weight on harming adversaries is required to rationalize the observed disruption.
